## What is being calculated

A game lists the worth of each subset of A, B and C in binary-mask order. There are eight entries, including a required zero for the empty set. An allocation supplies an exact rational number for each symbol. All inputs have fixed bounds and a closed schema. Worths are synthetic integers; they do not represent measured preferences, participant welfare, money or legal entitlements. The protocol rejects incomplete tables, unknown fields, repeated symbols, Boolean worths and invalid rational pairs. Standard-input runners also reject unsupported operations instead of guessing which calculation was intended.

The first session calculates tables, marginal contributions, interaction dividends, two allocation averages, dummy and null-symbol status, symmetry and three families of inequalities. The second checks allocation totals, standalone deficits, coalition excesses, membership of the supplied allocation in the core, a bounded integer-imputation list and the complement dual. Its remaining operations deliberately return explanation records, missing prerequisites and authority holds. The full definitions include the expected typed envelope and a digest, so a later reader can distinguish a changed explanation from a changed mathematical contract.

For the three-symbol game, the Shapley-style permutation allocation averages the marginal contribution of a symbol across all six possible orders. The uniform-subset operation instead averages four predecessor subsets for that symbol. These are different weighting choices. In the grand-unanimity fixture, where only the complete coalition has worth six, permutation averaging gives two to each symbol. Uniform-subset averaging gives three halves to each, totaling nine halves. The result is left exactly as calculated. The interface does not silently rescale one rule to imitate the other.

Another counterexample concerns core membership. The supplied grand-unanimity allocation is six, one and zero, totaling seven against grand worth six. Every coalition-excess inequality is satisfied, yet the allocation fails efficiency and is outside the core. The additive fixture supplies all six units to A even though B and C have standalone worths two and three; its audit retains the violated B, C and BC constraints. These concrete witnesses make the required conditions visible. They also show why a pass on one convenient check cannot be promoted to a pass on a larger claim.
