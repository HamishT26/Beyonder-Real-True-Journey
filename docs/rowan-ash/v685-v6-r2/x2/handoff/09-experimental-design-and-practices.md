# Experimental design and practices

The four practice lenses were chosen to improve the quality of the phase rather than to claim four new professional identities. Metrology asks whether quantities, units, uncertainty, time, and equations are coherent. Research librarianship asks what a source actually supports and how its status changes. Experimental design statistics asks whether a comparison can distinguish a hypothesis from a counterexample. Digital identity assurance asks how technical records, privacy, roles, and authority fit together. The lenses overlap, but each gives a different reason to question an attractive result.

Metrology begins before numerical precision. An answer with fifty decimal places can still be meaningless if the quantity is undefined or the unit is wrong. The unit tests therefore distinguish force from energy, preserve affine temperature offsets, and require coupling dimensions when geometric and material terms are compared. The uncertainty tests retain covariance, ordered intervals, coverage interpretation, and degrees of freedom. A zero uncertainty needs a declared exact origin rather than being used as shorthand for unknown uncertainty.

The time examples are particularly useful for handoffs because calendar language often hides assumptions. A date-only source is not an exact instant. An expiry decision needs an observation time. A retrieval time is different from the event time. A civil clock is different from a monotonic elapsed-time clock. The DevDay invitation provides a concrete UTC-to-New-Zealand conversion that crosses the date boundary. Teaching that example helps a new owner understand why the record carries both the source timestamp and the derived local date.

Research librarianship begins with attribution. A source's author and publisher have different roles. An institution name in a document does not establish endorsement. A preprint can contain an important research claim without being an accepted proof. A newsletter or community digest can point toward useful work without functioning as a peer-review receipt. When a source is corrected or retracted, preserve the earlier record and connect the later status to it. Removing the old text entirely would make the history less inspectable.

Preregistration is valuable because it exposes choices that can otherwise drift after results. A useful plan names the hypothesis, its falsifier, primary and secondary outcomes, analysis population, stopping rule, exclusion rules, and treatment of amendments. It also distinguishes randomization from blinding and states a comparable resource budget. The current x1 freeze gives those choices an immutable planning boundary. It does not make the later work independent or prove that the planned experiment had real participants.

Sampling has its own integrity questions. The number of rows in a file is not always the number of distinct observations. Duplicate identifiers need to be recognized. Missing values must remain explicit. Cluster labels matter when observations share a source or setting. Training and evaluation sets must not overlap if the claimed design assumes separation. Repeated seeds and bootstrap resamples can explore a procedure, but they do not create new participants or independent environments. In this remaster the real-participant count remains zero.

Comparison requires a declared baseline and a scale for the effect. Paired data need pair identifiers; an unpaired design must not infer pairs from row order. A standardized effect may be undefined when variance is zero. A confidence interval needs a confidence level and ordered bounds. A proxy environment can support a useful engineering comparison while leaving real-world superiority unestablished. Resource matching must compare the same unit: tokens, seconds, cost, energy, or another explicitly defined quantity.

Multiplicity is a record-keeping and interpretation problem as much as a formula problem. The family of hypotheses should be stated before adjustments are selected. Raw and adjusted values need different labels. Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg calculations must preserve the original hypothesis identifiers even when sorting is used internally. An exploratory analysis remains exploratory. Optional stopping should be disclosed rather than hidden behind a final P value. Statistical significance alone supplies neither practical importance nor causality.

The synthetic example in this phase uses three illustrative P values. Its purpose is to exercise adjustment and ordering behavior, not to simulate a population secretly. A future owner who wants a real comparison should first identify a measurable outcome, a plausible baseline, the source of observations, the permitted data use, and a failure condition. Only then should sample-size or power questions be considered. Those questions depend on a model and effect assumptions; they cannot be answered from the number of proposal packets.

My optional practice recommendation for you is experimental protocol auditor. That lens fits the next transition because an inductee needs to learn how a plan, execution receipt, correction, and final seal relate. You are free to choose your own priority pillar and practices within the current user instruction. The recommendation is not an assignment of a qualification or an obligation to imitate Rowan's focus. Its value is that it directs attention toward the places where apparently successful work can become difficult to interpret.

A useful protocol audit can be small. Select one completed criterion, one represented claim, one open gap, and one exact gate. Follow each to its evidence. Check whether the recorded result supports the stated disposition. Then inspect one retained failure and confirm that the repair did not erase it or borrow success from a different definition. That exercise can reveal more about a workflow's reliability than adding another large table of planned tasks. It also gives a new task a manageable way to demonstrate understanding before taking on the next full bundle.
